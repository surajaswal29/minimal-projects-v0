Seven glazes and why we stopped there

By Ines Hale12 September 20266 min read

When we opened the studio we had twenty-three glazes. There were test tiles on every windowsill, a spreadsheet with more tabs than rows, and a firing log that read like a list of small disappointments. Two years later we use seven. This is how we got there.

The trouble with choice

Every glaze behaves differently in the kiln. Some run, some crawl, some go matte if the kiln cools too slowly and glossy if it cools too fast. With twenty-three of them, every firing had a dozen variables we couldn't control. We were learning a little about many glazes and a lot about none.

We were learning a little about many glazes, and a lot about none.

So we set a rule: a glaze stays only if it earns a place on a pot we'd use every day. It has to be food safe, forgiving of thickness, and it has to look good on both our clays.

The seven

  • Celadon, a soft grey-green that pools darker in carved lines.
  • Tenmoku, iron-rich and nearly black, breaking to rust on the rims.
  • Shino, a warm cream with the odd flash of orange.
  • Cobalt, used sparingly, mostly on porcelain.
  • Ash, made from the wood-burner in the corner of the studio.
  • Rust, a satin red-brown we mixed by accident and then spent a month recreating.
  • Milk, a plain satin white for plates, because food looks best on it.

What we gained

Fewer glazes meant bigger batches, which meant more consistent results. It meant we could predict, within reason, what would come out of the kiln. And it gave the work a look: people started recognising a Kiln cup across a café.

We still test. There's a shelf of new tiles by the door. But a new glaze now has to replace one of the seven, not join them.
